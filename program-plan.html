01 · THE ARCHITECTURE

ONE THEME, TWO HALLS

Return to sport is the theme of SIC 2027. Every session answers one question from a different angle — how do we get this athlete back, safely and at full performance? Two parallel halls give the surgical and the rehabilitation audiences a full two days of depth each, while sports science and nutrition are woven into the sessions where they belong rather than separated into a track of their own. Joint plenary sessions keep it one conclave.

HALL ASurgery & Sports OrthopaedicsOrthopaedic & arthroscopy surgeons, sports physicians, PG trainees · ~55% of delegates
HALL BRehabilitation, Physiotherapy & Strength and ConditioningSports physiotherapists, S&C coaches, athletic trainers, sports scientists · ~45%

Room sizing: Hall A doubles as the plenary hall and must seat the full delegate count. Hall B needs roughly half that, plus clear floor space for the hands-on workshops.

01B · PLENARY SESSIONS, EXPANDED

WHAT EACH ONE ACTUALLY COVERS

The grid on page 3 only carries a title. Here is what a delegate is walking into for every plenary except Return to Sport: The Case, Together, whose full running order is on page 4.

Inauguration · 09:00, ~15 min

Formal opening — welcome address by the Chairman, brief remarks from Precision Orthopaedics LLP and Apollo Hospitals, and the Return to Sport theme framed for the two days ahead. Ceremonial, not content-bearing — the reason it can't move is protocol, not programme.

Opening keynote · 09:15, ~45 min

One marquee name, one talk, a career-spanning view of return to sport rather than a technique deep-dive — the tone-setter for everything that follows. Speaker TBC; this is the single highest-leverage name to confirm first, since it's heard by every delegate in the building.

Medical ethics & good clinical practice · 17:30, ~45 min

The TSMC-mandated session. Should cover informed consent in elective sports surgery, managing conflicts of interest with equipment and pharma sponsors — directly relevant given the orthobiologics content in Hall A and the equipment-sponsorship boundary in Section 7 — and documentation standards. Attendance is captured via the Declaration Proforma, which is why this has to be plenary rather than parallel.

Concussion — consensus-based practice · Day 2, 12:05, ~30 min

Built on the Amsterdam 2022 (6th International) consensus statement: the new visible signs mandating immediate removal from play, SCAT6/SCOAT6 in practice, and a graded return-to-play protocol realistic for Indian school and club sport without on-site physician cover.

Athlete fireside chat · Day 2, 12:35, ~35 min

A moderated conversation with a competitive athlete about their own injury and return journey — the patient's side of everything both halls have spent two days debating. Athlete TBC; the session most likely to get picked up by local press, so worth securing early.

Awards & valedictory · Day 2, 17:00, ~45 min

Best paper and best poster, one prize per track (surgical / rehab / science, per Section 7's decision on prize categories) so entries don't compete across disciplines. Chairman's closing remarks, formal close of the inaugural edition.

03 · HALL A

CASE-BASED DEBATE

Format — one real patient per block, not a survey of sub-topics. Chair presents an actual case, 2 min. Three surgeons give genuinely different views — 10 + 10 + 8 min, hard stops enforced by chairs. Physiotherapist, 10 min: the rehab arc for whichever path is chosen. S&C coach, 8 min: return to performance, not just return to play. Then 20–30 protected minutes (30 for the four blocks with the deepest three-way surgical splits — CB2, CB7, CB8, CB9; a firm 20 for the rest) — the floor puts the same case back to the whole panel: "he's in front of you now — what do you actually do?" Audience polls before and after. 68–78 minutes total; this is the Chairman's own design, and no published conference anywhere runs the exact same format — the closest precedents are AOSSM's case-based Instructional Course and ESSKA's Hot Topic Debate, both narrower. Pre-record all "live" surgery shown during a surgeon's slot — the operating surgeon narrates the recording live, which gives the teaching value without OT-link risk.

CB1 · Recurrent shoulder instability in the thrower/bowler FLAGSHIP — OPENS DAY 1

The case: 19-year-old fast bowler or throwing-sport athlete, recurrent anterior dislocation, glenoid bone loss on 3D-CT. Surgeon 1 argues arthroscopic Bankart + remplissage; Surgeon 2 argues Latarjet, and where the bone-loss threshold actually sits; Surgeon 3 makes the case for a free bone block, or argues against operating at all in-season. Physiotherapist: return-to-throwing progression — not return-to-sport in general, the throwing motion specifically. S&C coach: shoulder loading programme, scapular control, when full-velocity bowling is reintroduced. This is the case the organisers described first — every other block in Hall A follows its structure.

CB3 · Rotator cuff in the overhead athlete

The case: 33-year-old competitive-level badminton player, 8-month history of deep posterosuperior shoulder pain in the late-cocking/deceleration phase of the smash and serve; GIRD ~25° vs the non-dominant side, scapular dyskinesis, intact anterior stability and an intact biceps-labral complex (not the SLAP-dominant case CB12 covers). Four months of generic (non-internal-impingement-specific) physiotherapy gave partial relief. MR arthrogram: Ellman grade III (>50% thickness) articular-sided partial-thickness supraspinatus tear — a PASTA lesion — with preserved muscle bulk and no fatty infiltration. Surgeons debate a properly-run internal-impingement-specific conservative programme first (the prior physio trial wasn't the right one) vs arthroscopic debridement without formal repair (still the commonly taught default for these lesions) vs arthroscopic repair, transtendon technique preferred (2025 evidence shows debridement alone underperforms repair even at this grade; genuine equipoise remains between transtendon and tear-completion-and-repair technique once repair is chosen). Physiotherapist: GIRD-directed posterior-capsule stretching and scapular/kinetic-chain retraining, criteria- not calendar-based return to smash-specific loading. S&C coach: the core-endurance and scapular-stabiliser deficit that drove the overload in the first place, velocity-based return-to-smash progression.

Invited (subject to confirmation): Chair TBC · Physio & S&C: TBC from the invited pool
CB4 · The torn meniscus — save it or not

The case: posterior root tear with extrusion in a 56-year-old recreational athlete, varus alignment — a degenerative medial-root presentation, not the younger traumatic/ACL-associated lateral-root pattern. Physiotherapist: post-repair protected weight-bearing vs post-meniscectomy accelerated return. S&C coach: the load-management case for protecting a repaired meniscus long-term.

CB10 · Elbow OCD in the overhead athlete/gymnast

The case: 13-year-old competitive gymnast, 4 months of activity-related lateral elbow pain, intermittent catching, 15° terminal extension deficit; MRI shows a 12mm capitellar OCD lesion with a complete peripheral fluid line (unstable), open physes. Surgeon 1 argues conservative rest/bracing even against an unstable lesion — growth remaining and an intact cartilage cap still favour biological healing; Surgeon 2 argues arthroscopic debridement/microfracture — the lesion is unstable enough to act on now, and marrow stimulation is a lower-morbidity first surgical step; Surgeon 3 argues osteochondral autograft transplantation (OATS) — once a lesion clears the ≥10mm/unstable threshold, as this one does, anatomic restoration is preferred. Physiotherapist: kinetic-chain and valgus-loading mechanics, staged ROM — no RTS consensus exists yet, most protocols are still time-based (~6 months) rather than functional. S&C coach: youth-specific load management, pitch-count/gymnastics-load caps around peak-height-velocity windows, the early-specialisation argument. Fills the long-standing "elbow & overhead athlete" reserve slot with a concrete case.

CB11 · Acute traumatic full-thickness rotator cuff tear

The case: 58-year-old active patient, anterior shoulder dislocation from a fall, delayed MRI at 10 weeks shows a massive full-thickness supraspinatus + infraspinatus tear, stage 1–2 fatty infiltration, Hamada grade 1. Surgeon 1 argues primary repair despite the delay — traumatic tears behave differently from degenerative ones, and delayed repair hasn't been shown to worsen outcomes; Surgeon 2 argues joint-preserving reconstruction (SCR or balloon spacer) — the more cost-effective strategy in most massive-irreparable-tear models; Surgeon 3 argues primary reverse TSA in the active-but-aging patient — the definitive fix now, not a joint-preservation detour. Physiotherapist: three genuinely different rehab arcs depending on the path chosen. S&C coach: the loading ceiling forks sharply — full return to overhead/manual work after repair vs a permanent overhead-lifting restriction after RSA. Distinct from CB3's atraumatic, partial-thickness overhead-athlete case — same joint, different patient and different debate.

CB12 · SLAP tear in the overhead athlete

The case: 19-year-old Division-I-calibre pitcher, deep posterior shoulder pain in late cocking/acceleration, measurable velocity drop, positive O'Brien's and biceps-load-II, MR arthrogram confirms a type II SLAP lesion with an intact cuff — explicitly negative for instability. Surgeon 1 argues arthroscopic SLAP repair — the traditional position, conceding 40–80% pitcher RTP and a 7.5–12.5% revision rate; Surgeon 2 argues biceps tenodesis as the index procedure — gaining real ground even in throwers under 35, non-inferior on outcomes; Surgeon 3 argues conservative, rehab-first — ~40% of pro pitchers return on rehab alone. Physiotherapist: anchor-protective phase before resisted supination if repair is chosen, earlier and more aggressive biceps loading if tenodesis is chosen. S&C coach: scapular control, hip-trunk sequencing, velocity-based return-to-throwing testing. Distinct from CB1 (frank instability, positive apprehension) — schedule away from it in the running order so the "instability vs pain-generator" distinction registers.

CB13 · Acute ATFL repair — the first-time high-grade ankle sprain

The case: 24-year-old professional field-sport athlete, inversion injury 10 days before a season-defining tournament, complete mid-substance ATFL tear on MRI, intact CFL, no prior instability. Surgeon 1 argues functional rehab first — most first-time sprains, even high-grade, do well without surgery; Surgeon 2 argues acute anatomic repair without augmentation — restores native anatomy, augmentation over-treats a repairable tear; Surgeon 3 argues acute repair + internal-brace/suture-tape augmentation — superior load-to-failure, a meaningfully accelerated protected-loading timeline, increasingly favoured at elite level. Physiotherapist: the rehab arc diverges sharply by pathway. S&C coach: return-to-cutting/pivoting progression compressed for the augmented pathway, standard otherwise, watching for reinjury risk from schedule-driven acceleration. Distinct from CB6 (chronic/recurrent instability, a missed stress fracture) — this is the acute, first-event decision.

CB14 · Distal biceps tendon rupture

The case: 32-year-old competitive powerlifter, felt a pop with sharp antecubital pain during a max-effort deadlift, "Popeye" deformity, positive hook test, MRI confirms complete rupture with up to 2cm retraction, presents day 12 — still a clean window for direct primary repair. Nonoperative management isn't a credible position here — meaningful supination-strength deficits persist without repair — so all three surgeons operate and disagree only on technique/timing: Surgeon 1 single-incision cortical button — best strength/endurance data, delay past 4 weeks risks needing graft; Surgeon 2 double-incision transosseous fixation — historic gold standard, lower PIN-injury risk, trades for HO/synostosis risk and a second scar; Surgeon 3 suture-anchor fixation — biomechanically comparable to a button, easier revision if it fails. Physiotherapist: extension-block splinting the first 2 weeks, then criteria-based progression to axial loads. S&C coach: early isometric grip/shoulder work, blood-flow-restriction training, staged return-to-pull criteria — realistic timeline 4–6 months to heavy pulling, 9–12 to compete. Genuinely new territory, nothing like it on the current grid. Distal triceps rupture (the powerlifter/bodybuilder variant, linked to anabolic steroid use) is real but thinner — a one-line floor-debate coda here, not its own block for now.

04 · HALL B

REHABILITATION, PHYSIOTHERAPY & STRENGTH AND CONDITIONING

Format: 12–15 minute talks; blocks close with a moderated discussion rather than per-talk Q&A. Two ticketed, capped hands-on workshops — these are the reason physiotherapists pay to attend rather than watch online. Recruit S&C faculty, not only physiotherapists: an all-physio track loses the S&C audience by mid-morning of Day 1. Sports-science and nutrition content lives in this hall — see the two dedicated blocks below.

B1 · Criteria-based rehabilitation & the RTP decision

"Time is not a criterion" — what criteria-based actually means when you write the discharge note · what proportion of your athletes would truly pass a full test battery at 9 months post-ACLR · return to participation → sport → performance · psychological readiness (ACL-RSI) and the athlete whose knee passes but whose head doesn't · who owns the decision, and how it is documented. Closes by building a one-page RTP criteria sheet live from the floor.

DEBATE · No athlete should be cleared for pivoting sport before 9 months post-ACLR
B2 · Objective testing, screening & technology

Force plates in a 400-sq-ft clinic: the five countermovement-jump metrics worth reporting and the twenty that aren't · handheld dynamometry done properly — belt fixation, normalisation, minimum detectable change · isokinetics vs field testing in 2027 · movement screening after FMS: what survived the validity literature · markerless motion capture — good enough to change a clinical decision? · panel: "What would you buy first with ₹5 lakh?"

B3 · Tendinopathy & load-driven pain

06 · WHAT MAKES THIS PROGRAMME INDIAN

THE THREADS THAT MAKE IT OURS

A translated Western programme is the failure mode of a new conference. These threads run through both halls and give SIC 2027 an identity no international meeting can copy.

  • Kabaddi. Indian series data show ACL tears in 89.5% of injured players, 68.4% with meniscal involvement, 57.9% never returning to the sport, and 16.6 months mean time lost among those who do. That single dataset earns a case in the ACL, meniscus, hip/groin and rehabilitation sessions.
  • Cricket bowling workload and the fast bowler's back — across surgical, rehabilitation and load-management halls.
  • Heat and humidity. Heat policy, sweat sodium, acclimatisation. Non-negotiable for an Indian audience and absent from most imported programmes.
  • Vegetarian athlete nutrition — protein targets and the leucine threshold on Indian diets.
  • Doping. India has led WADA's violation table three years running. A reserve block on the 2026 Prohibited List for team clinicians is the most valuable single hour the conclave could add.
  • Rehabilitation without a lab. Most Indian clinics have no isokinetic dynamometer or force plate. Every rehab talk should state what to do without one.
  • Late presentation. Kabaddi ACL injuries present at a mean of ~14 months. Programme it as a clinical reality, not a footnote.

07 · DECISIONS FOR THE COMMITTEE

WHAT WE NEED SIGNED OFF

1 · Two days, or two plus a pre-conference day?

The research yields roughly three days of strong content. A 28 January pre-conference day would hold the hands-on workshops, any dry-lab work and the reserve blocks. Take this decision first — it determines the venue contract.

2 · Confirm the two-hall split, the hall titles, and the Hall A case-based format.

Hall A doubles as the plenary hall and must seat the full delegate count. Sports science and nutrition sit inside Hall B rather than in a hall of their own. Hall A's nine case blocks — one real patient, three surgeons, physiotherapist, S&C coach, floor debate — are the Chairman's own design and the meeting's actual point of difference; confirm the committee is aligned on running one deep case per hour rather than a survey of sub-topics before faculty outreach goes further.

3 · Faculty scale — Hall A specifically.

Hall A's 9 case blocks need 3 surgeons + 1 physiotherapist + 1 S&C coach each — 45 seats, fewer with deliberate reuse of a strong confirmed name across two blocks. Plus Hall B's own 55–70 lecture slots. Real, sourced candidates exist for every Hall A block; CB1 (the flagship shoulder case) is the best-staffed and should be confirmed first — a confirmed CB1 surgeon becomes the strongest recruiting tool for the rest. One seat remains genuinely open after two research passes: a surgeon or sports physician for CB8 who speaks specifically to fast-bowler lumbar stress fracture — this needs a personal introduction through the committee's own network, not further search. Full candidate list, with sources, in the committee's internal outreach document.

4 · Which debates run in Hall B.

Hall A's nine blocks are already debate by design. In Hall B, two debate-format lectures per day is the ceiling — more and they stop feeling like events.

5 · The ethics / GCP session stays as a plenary.

TSMC's Declaration Proforma requires it for CPD accreditation. Non-negotiable.

6 · Workshop ticketing.

Hands-on sessions must be capped and ticketed at registration with per-station rosters. Uncapped "hands-on" becomes a lecture with mats on the floor.

7 · Equipment sponsorship boundary.

Force plates and dynamometers will be vendor-supplied — tie them to an exhibitor package, but keep teaching faculty independent of the vendor and say so from the podium. This is the single biggest credibility risk in the rehabilitation track.

8 · Free-paper prizes per track.

So physiotherapy and nutrition entries don't compete against surgical case series.

Two research caveats, stated plainly. The ISAKOS, AOSSM and ESSKA programme pages blocked direct retrieval, so Hall A structure is reconstructed from search-indexed programme and abstract listings rather than read off the printed programme — worth checking the ESSKA 2026 highlight-lecture list before finalising. And IASMCON does not publish a session-level programme in machine-readable form, so the sports-science content now folded into Hall B is built from the consensus literature and Indian data rather than a competitor programme.
