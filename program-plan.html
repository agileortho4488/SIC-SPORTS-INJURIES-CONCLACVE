01 · THE ARCHITECTURE

ONE THEME, TWO HALLS

Return to sport is the theme of SIC 2027. Every session answers one question from a different angle — how do we get this athlete back, safely and at full performance? Two parallel halls give the surgical and the rehabilitation audiences a full two days of depth each, while sports science and nutrition are woven into the sessions where they belong rather than separated into a track of their own. Joint plenary sessions keep it one conclave.

HALL ASurgery & Sports OrthopaedicsOrthopaedic & arthroscopy surgeons, sports physicians, PG trainees · ~55% of delegates
HALL BRehabilitation, Physiotherapy & Strength and ConditioningSports physiotherapists, S&C coaches, athletic trainers, sports scientists · ~45%

Room sizing: Hall A doubles as the plenary hall and must seat the full delegate count. Hall B needs roughly half that, plus clear floor space for the hands-on workshops.

WHAT EACH ONE ACTUALLY COVERS

The grid on page 3 only carries a title. Here is what a delegate is walking into for every plenary except Return to Sport: The Case, Together, whose full running order is on page 4.

Inauguration · 09:00, ~15 min

Formal opening — welcome address by the Chairman, brief remarks from Precision Orthopaedics LLP and Apollo Hospitals, and the Return to Sport theme framed for the two days ahead. Ceremonial, not content-bearing — the reason it can't move is protocol, not programme.

Opening keynote · 09:15, ~45 min

One marquee name, one talk, a career-spanning view of return to sport rather than a technique deep-dive — the tone-setter for everything that follows. Speaker TBC; this is the single highest-leverage name to confirm first, since it's heard by every delegate in the building.

Medical ethics & good clinical practice · 17:30, ~45 min

The TSMC-mandated session. Should cover informed consent in elective sports surgery, managing conflicts of interest with equipment and pharma sponsors — directly relevant given the orthobiologics content in Hall A and the equipment-sponsorship boundary in Section 7 — and documentation standards. Attendance is captured via the Declaration Proforma, which is why this has to be plenary rather than parallel.

Concussion — consensus-based practice · Day 2, 12:05, ~30 min

Built on the Amsterdam 2022 (6th International) consensus statement: the new visible signs mandating immediate removal from play, SCAT6/SCOAT6 in practice, and a graded return-to-play protocol realistic for Indian school and club sport without on-site physician cover.

Athlete fireside chat · Day 2, 12:35, ~35 min

A moderated conversation with a competitive athlete about their own injury and return journey — the patient's side of everything both halls have spent two days debating. Athlete TBC; the session most likely to get picked up by local press, so worth securing early.

Awards & valedictory · Day 2, 17:00, ~45 min

Best paper and best poster, one prize per track (surgical / rehab / science, per Section 7's decision on prize categories) so entries don't compete across disciplines. Chairman's closing remarks, formal close of the inaugural edition.

03 · HALL A

CASE-BASED DEBATE

Format — one real patient per block, not a survey of sub-topics. Chair presents an actual case, 2 min. Three surgeons give genuinely different views — 10 + 10 + 8 min, hard stops enforced by chairs. Physiotherapist, 10 min: the rehab arc for whichever path is chosen. S&C coach, 8 min: return to performance, not just return to play. Then 20–30 protected minutes (30 for the four blocks with the deepest three-way surgical splits — CB2, CB7, CB8, CB9; a firm 20 for the rest) — the floor puts the same case back to the whole panel: "he's in front of you now — what do you actually do?" Audience polls before and after. 68–78 minutes total; this is the Chairman's own design, and no published conference anywhere runs the exact same format — the closest precedents are AOSSM's case-based Instructional Course and ESSKA's Hot Topic Debate, both narrower. Pre-record all "live" surgery shown during a surgeon's slot — the operating surgeon narrates the recording live, which gives the teaching value without OT-link risk.

CB1 · Recurrent shoulder instability in the thrower/bowler FLAGSHIP — OPENS DAY 1

The case: 19-year-old fast bowler, first anterior dislocation diving to field a shot 18 months ago, two further recurrences since; glenoid bone loss on 3D-CT (attributable to the fielding-collision trauma, not the bowling action itself, which drives GIRD/internal impingement instead). Surgeon 1 argues arthroscopic Bankart + remplissage; Surgeon 2 argues Latarjet, and where the bone-loss threshold actually sits; Surgeon 3 makes the case for a free bone block, or argues against operating at all in-season. Physiotherapist: return-to-throwing progression — not return-to-sport in general, the throwing motion specifically. S&C coach: shoulder loading programme, scapular control, when full-velocity bowling is reintroduced. This is the case the organisers described first — every other block in Hall A follows its structure.

CB3 · Rotator cuff in the overhead athlete

The case: 27-year-old national-circuit competitive tennis player (singles), 8-month history of deep posterosuperior shoulder pain in the late-cocking/deceleration phase of the serve; GIRD ~25° vs the non-dominant side, scapular dyskinesis, intact anterior stability and an intact biceps-labral complex (not the SLAP-dominant case CB12 covers). Four months of generic (non-internal-impingement-specific) physiotherapy gave partial relief. MR arthrogram: Ellman grade III (>50% thickness) articular-sided partial-thickness supraspinatus tear — a PASTA lesion — with preserved muscle bulk and no fatty infiltration. Surgeons debate a properly-run internal-impingement-specific conservative programme first (the prior physio trial wasn't the right one) vs arthroscopic debridement without formal repair (still the commonly taught default for these lesions) vs arthroscopic repair, transtendon technique preferred (2025 evidence shows debridement alone underperforms repair even at this grade; genuine equipoise remains between transtendon and tear-completion-and-repair technique once repair is chosen). Physiotherapist: GIRD-directed posterior-capsule stretching and scapular/kinetic-chain retraining, criteria- not calendar-based return to serve-specific loading. S&C coach: the core-endurance and scapular-stabiliser deficit that drove the overload in the first place, velocity-based return-to-serve progression.

CB10 · Elbow OCD in the overhead athlete/gymnast

The case: 13-year-old competitive gymnast, 4 months of activity-related lateral elbow pain, intermittent catching, 15° terminal extension deficit; MRI shows a 12mm capitellar OCD lesion with a complete peripheral fluid line (unstable), open physes. Surgeon 1 argues conservative rest/bracing even against an unstable lesion — growth remaining and an intact cartilage cap still favour biological healing; Surgeon 2 argues arthroscopic debridement/microfracture — the lesion is unstable enough to act on now, and marrow stimulation is a lower-morbidity first surgical step; Surgeon 3 argues osteochondral autograft transplantation (OATS) — once a lesion clears the ≥10mm/unstable threshold, as this one does, anatomic restoration is preferred. Physiotherapist: kinetic-chain and valgus-loading mechanics, staged ROM — no RTS consensus exists yet, most protocols are still time-based (~6 months) rather than functional. S&C coach: youth-specific load management, pitch-count/gymnastics-load caps around peak-height-velocity windows, the early-specialisation argument. Fills the long-standing "elbow & overhead athlete" reserve slot with a concrete case.

CB11 · Acute traumatic full-thickness rotator cuff tear

The case: 58-year-old active patient, anterior shoulder dislocation from a fall, delayed MRI at 10 weeks shows a massive full-thickness supraspinatus + infraspinatus tear, stage 1–2 fatty infiltration, Hamada grade 1. Surgeon 1 argues primary repair despite the delay — traumatic tears behave differently from degenerative ones, and delayed repair hasn't been shown to worsen outcomes; Surgeon 2 argues joint-preserving reconstruction (SCR or balloon spacer) — the more cost-effective strategy in most massive-irreparable-tear models; Surgeon 3 argues primary reverse TSA in the active-but-aging patient — the definitive fix now, not a joint-preservation detour. Physiotherapist: three genuinely different rehab arcs depending on the path chosen. S&C coach: the loading ceiling forks sharply — full return to overhead/manual work after repair vs a permanent overhead-lifting restriction after RSA. Distinct from CB3's atraumatic, partial-thickness overhead-athlete case — same joint, different patient and different debate.

CB12 · SLAP tear in the overhead athlete

The case: 19-year-old Division-I-calibre pitcher, deep posterior shoulder pain in late cocking/acceleration, measurable velocity drop, positive O'Brien's and biceps-load-II, MR arthrogram confirms a type II SLAP lesion with an intact cuff — explicitly negative for instability. Surgeon 1 argues arthroscopic SLAP repair — the traditional position, conceding 40–80% pitcher RTP and a 7.5–12.5% revision rate; Surgeon 2 argues biceps tenodesis as the index procedure — gaining real ground even in throwers under 35, non-inferior on outcomes; Surgeon 3 argues conservative, rehab-first — ~40% of pro pitchers return on rehab alone. Physiotherapist: anchor-protective phase before resisted supination if repair is chosen, earlier and more aggressive biceps loading if tenodesis is chosen. S&C coach: scapular control, hip-trunk sequencing, velocity-based return-to-throwing testing. Distinct from CB1 (frank instability, positive apprehension) — schedule away from it in the running order so the "instability vs pain-generator" distinction registers.

CB13 · Acute ATFL repair — the first-time high-grade ankle sprain

The case: 24-year-old professional field-sport athlete, inversion injury 10 days before a season-defining tournament, complete mid-substance ATFL tear on MRI, intact CFL, no prior instability. Surgeon 1 argues functional rehab first — most first-time sprains, even high-grade, do well without surgery; Surgeon 2 argues acute anatomic repair without augmentation — restores native anatomy, augmentation over-treats a repairable tear; Surgeon 3 argues acute repair + internal-brace/suture-tape augmentation — superior load-to-failure, a meaningfully accelerated protected-loading timeline, increasingly favoured at elite level. Physiotherapist: the rehab arc diverges sharply by pathway. S&C coach: return-to-cutting/pivoting progression compressed for the augmented pathway, standard otherwise, watching for reinjury risk from schedule-driven acceleration. Distinct from CB6 (chronic/recurrent instability, a missed stress fracture) — this is the acute, first-event decision.

CB14 · Distal biceps tendon rupture

The case: 32-year-old competitive powerlifter, felt a pop with sharp antecubital pain during a max-effort deadlift, "Popeye" deformity, positive hook test, MRI confirms complete rupture with up to 2cm retraction, presents day 12 — still a clean window for direct primary repair. Nonoperative management isn't a credible position here — meaningful supination-strength deficits persist without repair — so all three surgeons operate and disagree only on technique/timing: Surgeon 1 single-incision cortical button — best strength/endurance data, delay past 4 weeks risks needing graft; Surgeon 2 double-incision transosseous fixation — historic gold standard, lower PIN-injury risk, trades for HO/synostosis risk and a second scar; Surgeon 3 suture-anchor fixation — biomechanically comparable to a button, easier revision if it fails. Physiotherapist: extension-block splinting the first 2 weeks, then criteria-based progression to axial loads. S&C coach: early isometric grip/shoulder work, blood-flow-restriction training, staged return-to-pull criteria — realistic timeline 4–6 months to heavy pulling, 9–12 to compete. Genuinely new territory, nothing like it on the current grid. Distal triceps rupture (the powerlifter/bodybuilder variant, linked to anabolic steroid use) is real but thinner — a one-line floor-debate coda here, not its own block for now. Second coda (Sep 2026): pectoralis major rupture was researched and rejected as its own block (overlaps this one, and non-operative care in athletes is settled) — ask the floor: "would you still operate on a chronic, retracted pec major tear in a steroid-using bodybuilder?"

CB16 · Achilles tendon rupture

The case: 34-year-old competitive club badminton player, push-off for a backhand lunge, audible pop and the classic "felt kicked from behind," presents day 6. Palpable 4cm gap, positive Thompson test, resting plantarflexion asymmetry; ultrasound shows full-thickness rupture 4cm from the insertion, 12mm gap in equinus. Surgeon 1 argues non-operative management with accelerated functional rehab — identical 12-month function, and it avoids infection and nerve injury entirely; Surgeon 2 argues open repair — roughly ten-fold re-rupture reduction, and an athlete who must push off cannot absorb a ~6% career-ending risk; Surgeon 3 argues percutaneous/minimally-invasive repair — re-rupture equal to open, infection near-zero, priced in sural nerve palsy. The 2022 NEJM three-arm trial (554 patients) is the spine of the block: no difference in patient-reported outcome across all three arms, but re-rupture 6.2% / 0.6% / 0.6% and 5.2% nerve injury in the MIS arm. Physiotherapist: functional bracing, heel-wedge weaning, tendon elongation monitored via heel-rise height — and the honest finding that accelerated vs delayed rehab showed no outcome difference, so what actually drives recovery? S&C coach: the plantarflexor deficit that outlasts every protocol; heel-rise endurance and reactive-strength criteria before return to court.

THE FLOOR, TOGETHER · identical function, ten-fold re-rupture difference, zero difference in patient-reported score — who owns that decision, and does your answer change at day 6 versus day 21?Why badminton, and a warning for chairs: in a 140-case badminton injury series Achilles rupture was the second commonest diagnosis (13.9%) — this is also the block that brings India's second sport into the programme and the only case landing in the 30–55 age band. Note: UKSTAR is not an operative-vs-non-operative trial — it randomised patients already on non-operative care between cast and functional brace. Correct any panellist who cites it as the op-vs-non-op evidence.
CB17 · Patellofemoral instability — when do you add bone?

The case: 17-year-old female state-level volleyball middle-blocker, third lateral patellar dislocation, this one non-contact on landing. Apprehension at 30°, positive J-sign; MRI shows Dejour C trochlear dysplasia, TT-TG 21mm, Caton-Deschamps 1.35, medial patellar facet chondral flap. Skeletally mature. Surgeon 1 argues isolated MPFL reconstruction — the bony numbers are borderline, and MPFL-R alone returns 92.8% to sport with 1.9% recurrence; Surgeon 2 argues MPFL-R plus tibial tubercle osteotomy — TT-TG 21 with patella alta is exactly the risk profile the definitive RCT excluded; Surgeon 3 argues trochleoplasty — the only procedure correcting the primary pathology (pooled redislocation 0.35% across 927 patients), but a demanding operation on an already cartilage-injured knee. Physiotherapist: hip abductor and external-rotator loading rather than "VMO activation," brace weaning, kinesiophobia, criterion-based staging. S&C coach: frontal-plane landing control in a jumping athlete, eccentric quadriceps capacity, growth/maturation load management in the female athlete.

THE FLOOR, TOGETHER · a 1mm difference in a measurement with known inter-observer variability decides whether this girl gets an osteotomy — show me your threshold, and your evidence for it.Framing note for the chair: do NOT build this as MPFL reconstruction versus rehabilitation — that question is closing (3-year RCT: 16.7% vs 53.6% persistent instability). The live question is when to add bone, and it is live precisely because that same trial excluded Dejour D dysplasia and TT-TG >20mm — the patient this vignette describes.
CB18 · Multiligament knee injury / knee dislocation

The case: 24-year-old state-level kabaddi raider, ankle-hold by two defenders during a raid, forced hyperextension with valgus, unable to weight-bear; reduced on the mat, presents day 3 at a district hospital, referred day 6. Lachman 3+, posterior sag with 3+ posterior drawer, valgus laxity in full extension, dial test negative — Schenck KD-IIIM. MRI: ACL and PCL mid-substance ruptures, superficial MCL proximal peel-off, lateral meniscus posterior-root avulsion. Neurovascular status is deliberately designed: pulses palpable, ABI 1.0, but a dense common peroneal palsy with foot drop — including the nerve while excluding arterial injury is what keeps three positions live, since a vascular injury collapses the case into one algorithm. Surgeon 1 reconstructs everything in one stage this week — reconstruction beats repair on arthrofibrosis (5.4% vs 11.6%) and failure (2.9% vs 5.7%); Surgeon 2 braces/ex-fixes now and reconstructs at 3–6 weeks — no proven superiority of early over delayed, early surgery raises stiffness risk, and this is what most surgeons in resource-constrained settings actually do (argued as a deliberate choice, not a compromise); Surgeon 3 repairs the MCL peel-off with internal-brace augmentation and owns the nerve — must defend the brace against an Indian systematic review finding it "as effective as not using it," and is the only one addressing the foot drop (AFO and observe vs early neurolysis vs planned tendon transfer). Physiotherapist: the stiffness-versus-laxity trap, which is genuine — push motion and you fight arthrofibrosis, protect the knee and the PCL graft creeps into posterior sag. S&C coach: return to performance where Tegner falls 7.12 to 4.59, rebuilding hyperextension-tolerant landing without a dynamometer or force plate.

THE FLOOR, TOGETHER · pulses are normal, the foot is dropped, there's no CT angiogram and no PCL allograft in the building — do you fix this knee this week, or splint it and send him 400km?Overlap control with CB2 is mandatory — same sport, same joint. Separate by pathway, not pathology: CB2 is elective, late-presenting, single-ligament, rotational-laxity (graft choice and LET); CB18 is acute, bicruciate, limb-and-nerve, resource-constrained. The chair should rule "hamstring vs quad vs BTB" and "LET" out of order in CB18, and "knee dislocation" out of order in CB2. Do not schedule them adjacent. Floor coda (Sep 2026): isolated PCL was researched and rejected as its own block — ask "if this PCL tear were isolated, would you reconstruct it at all?"

CB15 · Revision & failed surgery — the failed ACL reconstruction

The case: 21-year-old male state-level football midfielder. Index surgery at 19: 8mm hamstring graft plus partial medial meniscectomy; back to matches at 7 months. Non-contact cutting injury in a match two years later. Lachman 2B, pivot shift grade 3; dial test negative, PCL and collaterals intact, neutral alignment. CT: femoral tunnel 12mm, high and shallow (a new anatomic tunnel would breach its back wall); tibial tunnel widened to 14mm; posterior tibial slope 13°; medial meniscus posterior horn deficient. The failure is multifactorial on purpose. Settled, not debated: revise with autograft (allograft re-ruptured more in MARS, and is scarce in India) — all three surgeons use BTB autograft. Surgeon 1 argues one-stage anatomic revision plus LET, leaving the slope — in patients 25 or under LET was associated with fewer revisions regardless of slope; Surgeon 2 argues one-stage slope-reducing osteotomy plus revision, no LET — slope ≥12° was the strongest 20-year predictor of repeat injury, though a 46-expert Delphi could not agree a threshold and hardware reoperation ran at 26%; Surgeon 3 argues two stages — graft both tunnels, revise at 4–6 months: lower failure (OR 0.12) but worse KOOS and more anterior knee pain. Physiotherapist: fear of reinjury is the commonest reason revision patients don't return (28%); track ACL-RSI from before surgery. S&C coach: no return before 9 months; graded match minutes.

THE FLOOR, TOGETHER · if 46 experts couldn't agree a slope threshold, is 13° a reason to cut bone in a 21-year-old at his first revision — or does LET do it more cheaply?Overlap control with CB2: graft choice is fixed (BTB for all) and ruled out of order. The 12mm / 14mm / 13° values are template figures inside the argued ranges — replace with a real patient's before presenting.
CB19 · The syndesmosis that might be unstable

The case: 23-year-old male state-league footballer; tackle with the foot planted, forcing dorsiflexion and external rotation. Squeeze and external-rotation stress tests positive, anterior drawer negative. Weight-bearing radiographs: no fracture or frank widening, medial clear space at the upper limit of normal. MRI: AITFL torn, interosseous ligament partial, PITFL intact, superficial deltoid partial, deep deltoid in continuity, ATFL intact. No weight-bearing CT. Borderline because clinical tests cannot separate stable from unstable, and a deltoid injury plus partial syndesmotic tear is unstable in cadavers. Settled, not debated: stable injuries do well without surgery; suture-button beats screw (meta-analysis of 16 RCTs) — so the fixation device is not the debate. Surgeon 1 argues “don't operate on a scan” — boot and criteria-based rehab; return to sport ~98% for both non-operative and suture-button care in elite athletes; Surgeon 2 argues arthroscopy is the test — fix only if fibular translation is ≥2mm, leave the deltoid; Surgeon 3 argues rebuild it — suture-button plus AITFL augmentation plus deltoid repair, because only combined repair restored rotational stability (cadaveric evidence, which the floor should press). Physiotherapist: the dorsiflexion lunge as the tracking test; caution with early external-rotation loading after deltoid repair. S&C coach: straight-line, then cutting, contact last; watch for fibular pain after suture-button fixation.

THE FLOOR, TOGETHER · if non-operative and suture-button groups return at the same rate, what justifies rebuilding a deltoid that isn't fully torn?Why football, and India: football is the only India-played sport with published syndesmosis evidence; none was found for cricket, kabaddi, hockey or badminton. Overlap control: external-rotation mechanism, ATFL intact — CB13's lateral-ligament and CB6's chronic-instability debates are out of order.
CB20 · The subtle Lisfranc: fix, fuse or flex?

The case: 21-year-old female state-level basketball player; landed from a rebound on an opponent's foot. Plantar midfoot bruising, painful piano-key test at the second ray. Non-weight-bearing radiographs normal; standing bilateral films show the first cuneiform–second metatarsal gap 2.5mm wider than the other side; MRI shows a torn plantar Lisfranc ligament and injured intercuneiform ligament, no fracture. Settled, not debated: stable undisplaced injuries do well without surgery; residual displacement over 2mm predicts worse careers. Surgeon 1 argues rigid fixation with planned hardware removal — the established athlete pathway, return at ~7.5 months, and a 10-year trial found bridge plating held up against fusion; Surgeon 2 argues primary partial fusion — the only RCT in purely ligamentous injuries favoured fusion and it avoids a second operation, but no trial studied athletes; Surgeon 3 argues flexible suture-button fixation — elite athletes back at 12–16 weeks, but case-series evidence only and the early advantage fades by one year. The JBJS 2026 commentary is titled “Fix or Fuse: The Lisfranc Debate Persists”. Physiotherapist: weight-bearing timelines differ completely by pathway; calf and intrinsic foot capacity, hop symmetry. S&C coach: landing and cutting mechanics, footwear, rebuilding jump volume.

THE FLOOR, TOGETHER · with no trial in athletes, a fusion that removes the second operation — or a motion-preserving repair that removes the certainty?Turf toe coda (rejected as its own block — under 2% need surgery): “which forefoot and midfoot injuries must have weight-bearing or stress imaging before a return-to-play decision?” Overlap control: CB6 is also basketball — the female patient and midfoot pathology keep them distinct, but don't schedule them adjacent.

CB21 · The focal cartilage defect in the young footballer

The case: 24-year-old male semi-professional footballer; non-contact twist 8 months ago, knee swollen but stable; medial pain and effusion after every match since; 4 months of supervised rehab failed. MRI: 18 × 14mm (~2.5cm²) full-thickness defect of the central weight-bearing medial femoral condyle, well-shouldered, subchondral plate intact; ACL and both menisci intact. Neutral alignment, so osteotomy is not indicated. At 2.5cm² all three positions are legitimate — a clash of schools, not a size triage. Settled, not debated: plain microfracture is effectively obsolete for footballers per the 2025–26 FIFA/ICRS/Aspetar consensus, so nobody defends it. Surgeon 1 argues single-stage osteochondral autograft transfer — hyaline cartilage and the fastest return (93% back to prior level vs 52% after microfracture in athletes), but 2.5cm² needs several plugs; Surgeon 2 argues two-stage cell-based cartilage repair — better 10-year function in an RCT, favoured for players prioritising the long term, but two operations and the cost; Surgeon 3 argues single-stage marrow stimulation under a cell-free scaffold — the affordability case, on low-certainty evidence, and must concede the consensus rating. Physiotherapist: each construct loads differently; effusion-guided, criteria-based progression. S&C coach: graded return to high-speed running, deceleration and impact.

THE FLOOR, TOGETHER · does a scaffold-augmented marrow stimulation today burn the bridge to cell-based repair tomorrow?India reality check — corrects an earlier assumption: cell-based cartilage repair is available in India (regulator-authorised, in use since 2008) — cost, not availability, is the barrier. Fresh osteochondral allograft is not a realistic Indian option and is excluded. Overlap control: not CB4 (56, varus, degenerative) or CB10 (adolescent OCD). Use generic names on slides.
CB22 · Grade III AC joint dislocation: IIIA or IIIB?

The case: 24-year-old male state-level road cyclist; crashed onto the point of the shoulder; seen at day 10. Prominent reducible distal clavicle, tender AC and coracoclavicular ligaments, pain on cross-body adduction. Zanca views: coracoclavicular distance ~80% greater than the other side (grade III); axillary view excludes grade IV; the cross-body view shows the clavicle overriding the acromion — the radiographic sign of IIIB, though at day 10 nobody can say whether the scapular dysfunction will settle. Surgeon 1 argues non-operative first — a meta-analysis of five RCTs found no Constant-score difference at 2 years, and surgery added minor complications; Surgeon 2 argues early arthroscopic coracoclavicular stabilisation — the “no difference” result is statistically fragile and came from trials averaging age 39, while a younger RCT favoured surgery on UCLA score and cosmesis, and return to play reaches 98.7%; Surgeon 3 argues two-plane stabilisation — an overriding clavicle is horizontal instability that vertical-only fixation leaves untreated, though its RCT showed no clinical difference at a year. Physiotherapist: what rehab must show, and by when, before calling it IIIA or IIIB. S&C coach: handlebar and aero-position loading, early indoor riding, re-crash risk.

THE FLOOR, TOGETHER · the meta-analyses pool trials of 39-year-olds — is a trial of rehab evidence-based for this 24-year-old, or does it waste the window for fixation?Why cycling: 51 of 106 AC dislocations in a Danish cohort came from cycling, peaking at age 20–24 (evidence is Danish and American, not Indian). Note: this is the fifth shoulder-region block — if a shoulder reserve is promoted, CB22 fits an Indian audience better than CB12's baseball pitcher. Raise low-cost hook-plate fixation from the floor.

06 · WHAT MAKES THIS PROGRAMME INDIAN

THE THREADS THAT MAKE IT OURS

A translated Western programme is the failure mode of a new conference. These threads run through both halls and give SIC 2027 an identity no international meeting can copy.

  • Kabaddi. Indian series data show ACL tears in 89.5% of injured players, 68.4% with meniscal involvement, 57.9% never returning to the sport, and 16.6 months mean time lost among those who do. That single dataset earns a case in the ACL, meniscus, hip/groin and rehabilitation sessions.
  • Cricket bowling workload and the fast bowler's back — across surgical, rehabilitation and load-management halls.
  • Heat and humidity. Heat policy, sweat sodium, acclimatisation. Non-negotiable for an Indian audience and absent from most imported programmes.
  • Vegetarian athlete nutrition — protein targets and the leucine threshold on Indian diets.
  • Doping. India has led WADA's violation table three years running. A reserve block on the 2026 Prohibited List for team clinicians is the most valuable single hour the conclave could add.
  • Rehabilitation without a lab. Most Indian clinics have no isokinetic dynamometer or force plate. Every rehab talk should state what to do without one.
  • Late presentation. Kabaddi ACL injuries present at a mean of ~14 months. Programme it as a clinical reality, not a footnote.

4 · Which debates run in Hall B.

Hall A's nine blocks are already debate by design. In Hall B, two debate-format lectures per day is the ceiling — more and they stop feeling like events.

5 · The ethics / GCP session stays as a plenary.

TSMC's Declaration Proforma requires it for CPD accreditation. Non-negotiable.

6 · Workshop ticketing.

Hands-on sessions must be capped and ticketed at registration with per-station rosters. Uncapped "hands-on" becomes a lecture with mats on the floor.

7 · Equipment sponsorship boundary.

Force plates and dynamometers will be vendor-supplied — tie them to an exhibitor package, but keep teaching faculty independent of the vendor and say so from the podium. This is the single biggest credibility risk in the rehabilitation track.

8 · Free-paper prizes per track.

So physiotherapy and nutrition entries don't compete against surgical case series.

Two research caveats, stated plainly. The ISAKOS, AOSSM and ESSKA programme pages blocked direct retrieval, so Hall A structure is reconstructed from search-indexed programme and abstract listings rather than read off the printed programme — worth checking the ESSKA 2026 highlight-lecture list before finalising. And IASMCON does not publish a session-level programme in machine-readable form, so the sports-science content now folded into Hall B is built from the consensus literature and Indian data rather than a competitor programme.
