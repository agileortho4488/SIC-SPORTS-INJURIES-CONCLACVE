# SIC 2027 — Two-Hall Scientific Program Plan

**Hyderabad Sports Injuries Conclave · 29th & 30th January 2027**
Draft for the Scientific Committee · prepared from a review of IASCON 2025/26, IASMCON 2025, ISKSAA, ISAKOS 2025, AOSSM 2025, ESSKA 2026, IFSPT/World Physiotherapy, NSCA, and the IOC/WADA/ACSM consensus literature. Every source is listed at the end of each hall section.

> **Structure finalised:** two halls, decided by the Chairman. Return to sport is the through-line. Sports science, nutrition and athlete health are **not** a third room — they sit inside Hall B, next to the people who act on them. This document was originally drafted as three halls; section 5 explains what that costs.
>
> **Hall A format finalised (this revision):** the Chairman's own design — a single clinical case run by three surgeons with differing views, then a physiotherapist, then a strength & conditioning coach, closing with a floor debate among all of them on how to actually manage that patient. Section 3 rebuilds Hall A around nine of these case blocks. ## 1. The architecture — why two halls

One hall forces a compromise programme: surgeons sit through nutrition talks, physiotherapists sit through arthroscopic technique. Three halls solve that but split a first-year conclave too thin — a 20% room is a room that looks empty in photographs, and it puts nutrition and sports science in front of nutritionists only, which is precisely the audience that already agrees.

Two halls keep the split where the professions genuinely diverge — operative vs non-operative — and put the science in front of the clinicians who have to act on it.

| | Hall A | Hall B |
|---|---|---|
| **Title** | Surgery & Sports Orthopaedics | Rehab, Physiotherapy, S&C & Sports Science |
| **Audience** | Orthopaedic & arthroscopy surgeons, sports physicians, ortho PGs | Sports physiotherapists, S&C coaches, athletic trainers, nutritionists, exercise physiologists, team doctors, physio PGs |
| **Expected share** | ~45% | ~55% |
| **Room need** | Largest (also hosts plenary) | Large + floor space for hands-on |

**Sizing rule:** Hall A doubles as the plenary hall, so it must seat the full delegate count. Hall B now carries the larger share of delegates — size it at roughly 55–60% of Hall A, not the 40% a secondary room would need.

**What each of these actually covers** — the grid only carries a title; here is what a delegate is walking into:

- **Inauguration (09:00, ~15 min).** Formal opening — welcome address by the Chairman, brief remarks from Precision Orthopaedics LLP and Apollo Hospitals, and the Return to Sport theme framed for the two days ahead. Ceremonial, not content-bearing — the reason it can't move is protocol, not programme.

- **Opening keynote (09:15, ~45 min).** One marquee name, one talk, a career-spanning view of return to sport rather than a technique deep-dive — the tone-setter for everything that follows. Speaker TBC; this is the single highest-leverage confirmed name to chase first, since it's heard by literally every delegate in the building.

- **Return-to-Sport: The Case, Together (12:45).** Full running order already specified below — the proof-of-concept for Hall A's nine case blocks.

- **Medical ethics & good clinical practice (17:30, ~45 min).** The TSMC-mandated session. Content should cover informed consent in elective sports surgery, managing conflicts of interest with equipment and pharma sponsors — directly relevant given the orthobiologics content in CB and the equipment-sponsorship boundary flagged in Section 7 — and documentation standards. Attendance is captured via the Declaration Proforma, which is *why* this has to be plenary rather than parallel.

- **Concussion — consensus-based practice (Day 2, 12:05, ~30 min).** Built on the Amsterdam 2022 (6th International) consensus statement: the new visible signs mandating immediate removal from play, SCAT6/SCOAT6 in practice, and a graded return-to-play protocol realistic for Indian school and club sport without on-site physician cover.

- **Athlete fireside chat (Day 2, 12:35, ~35 min).** A moderated conversation with a competitive athlete about their own injury and return journey — the patient's side of everything both halls have spent two days debating. Athlete TBC; this is the session most likely to get picked up by local press, so worth securing early.

- **Awards & valedictory (Day 2, 17:00, ~45 min).** Best paper and best poster, one prize per track (surgical / rehab / science, per Section 7's decision on prize categories so entries don't compete across disciplines), chairman's closing remarks, formal close of the inaugural edition.

> **Return-to-Sport: The Case, Together — the exact running order.** This is the same template as every Hall A case block (Section 3), run once for the whole conclave before Hall A splits into parallel cases:
>
> A real, complex case with genuine disagreement built in -- e.g. a national-level athlete 7 months post-ACLR, cleared by imaging, failed on hop-test symmetry. Chair presents it (2 min). **Surgeon** -- was the surgery/graft choice right, would they have done anything differently in hindsight (8 min). **Physiotherapist** -- the criteria this athlete actually failed and why "7 months" was never the real clock (10 min). **S&C coach** -- what "return to performance" demands that "return to play" doesn't test for (8 min). **Nutritionist** -- 3 min, only if directly relevant to the case (e.g. an energy-availability angle). Then **12 minutes, floor open to all four plus the athlete themselves if present**: "she's in front of you right now, symmetric on paper, not right in the coach's eyes -- what do you actually tell her?" This is the session every delegate in the building watches at once, and it is the proof-of-concept for the nine case blocks that follow it in Hall A.

## 3. Hall A -- Case-Based Debate

**This is the Chairman's format, not a copy of anyone else's programme.** No published conference anywhere in the evidence base runs a surgeon-physio-S&C same-case debate -- the closest precedents are AOSSM's case-based Instructional Course (moderator presents a case to a 4-6 person panel, <=10-min talks, 15 min mandatory audience Q&A) and ISAKOS's "Battle" format (opposed short talks + moderated decision). SIC 2027 combines both and adds the S&C voice that neither one has. That is the meeting's actual point of difference -- sell it as such in the brochure.

**Block template (68-78 min, 9 blocks across two days):**

| Slot | Time | Who | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 min | Chair | Presents the real case -- age, sport, mechanism, findings. One patient, not an abstract topic. |
| 2 | 10 min | Surgeon 1 | Their surgical read of the case and why |
| 3 | 10 min | Surgeon 2 | A genuinely different surgical view -- different technique, different threshold, or "I wouldn't operate" |
| 4 | 8 min | Surgeon 3 | Third angle -- revision/salvage risk, a technical pearl, or the paediatric/adolescent variant of the same injury |
| 5 | 10 min | Physiotherapist | The rehab arc for whichever path is chosen -- criteria, not calendar dates |
| 6 | 8 min | S&C coach | Return-to-performance, not just return-to-play -- loading, testing, the training-age question |
| 7 | 20-30 min | **All five, floor open** | Chair puts the SAME case back to the panel: "he's in front of you now -- what do you actually do?" Audience polls before and after. |

Talks are capped at 10 min because every major society (AOSSM, ESSKA, ISAKOS) converges on that ceiling for exactly this reason: past 10 minutes a "talk" becomes a lecture and the panel loses its case-based feel. The closing debate is **protected time, guaranteed 20-30 minutes** -- chairs must not let slot overruns eat into it; it is the reason delegates chose this block over the alternative track in Hall B. Blocks with the deepest genuine three-way surgical splits get the full 30 (CB2, CB7, CB8, CB9); the rest get a firm 20 (CB1, CB3, CB4, CB5, CB6). This is why the template's total grew from a flat 63 min to 68-78 min -- see Section 2's master grid for how that was absorbed into the day (mostly by removing slack that already existed between blocks, so Day 1 finishes only 2 min later and Day 2 finishes 30 min later than the previous draft).

**The nine case blocks:**

**CB1 - Recurrent shoulder instability in the thrower/bowler** *(flagship -- opens Day 1, 10:00)* -- 19-year-old fast bowler, first anterior dislocation diving to field a shot 18 months ago, two further recurrences since; recurrent anterior instability with glenoid bone loss on 3D-CT. *Mechanism corrected (Aug 2026) -- glenoid bone loss is driven by repeated high-energy dislocation (contact/collision-type trauma), not by the bowling action itself, which produces GIRD/internal impingement/SLAP pathology instead (the territory CB3 and CB12 already cover) -- kept the flagship case's cricket identity, since every fast bowler also fields, but attributed the bone-loss-producing trauma to fielding collisions rather than to bowling.* Surgical debate: arthroscopic Bankart + remplissage vs Latarjet vs free bone block, and where the bone-loss threshold actually sits. Physio: return-to-throwing progression, not just return-to-sport. S&C: shoulder loading programme, scapular control, when to reintroduce full-velocity bowling. *This is the case the organisers described first -- the template every other block follows.*

**CB2 - ACL rupture in the pivoting athlete** *(Day 1, 11:23, 30-min floor debate)* -- 23-year-old female kabaddi or football player, complete ACL tear, meniscal involvement (age matches the competitive-kabaddi ACLR cohort literature, mean ~24, rather than general-population ACL-injury age ~29). *Sex specified female (Aug 2026) -- ACL injury risk runs roughly 1.5-3x higher in female athletes depending on sport, so of all 14 cases this was the one where an unstated male default was actively at odds with the epidemiology. It was also, before this change, one of fourteen consecutive male-or-unstated cases; women's kabaddi is well established in India, so this costs the case nothing in realism.* Surgical debate: graft choice (hamstring vs quadriceps tendon vs BTB), repair-with-augmentation for the right 5%, lateral extra-articular tenodesis or not. Physio: criteria-based RTP, not a 9-month calendar date. S&C: the kabaddi-specific loading pattern -- India's own injury data (89.5% ACL tear rate in injured kabaddi players) makes this the most locally relevant case in the programme.

**CB4 - The torn meniscus -- save it or not** *(Day 1, 15:34, 20-min floor debate)* -- posterior root tear with extrusion in a 56-year-old recreational athlete, varus alignment. *Age corrected (Aug 2026) from 30 to 56 -- medial posterior root tears with extrusion are a degenerative, older-patient pathology (mean age 55-62 across multiple cohorts); the traumatic/young-patient version is a lateral root tear, almost always ACL-associated, which would blur this case into CB2.* Surgical debate: repair vs partial meniscectomy vs "meniscectomy is not a crime" -- the cases where resection is genuinely right, which in current evidence increasingly means the older/higher-BMI/varus patient this vignette now describes, not a head-to-head efficacy fight (repair beats meniscectomy on OA progression and TKA conversion in appropriate candidates -- the real debate is patient-selection thresholds, not which procedure "wins"). Physio: post-repair protected weight-bearing vs post-meniscectomy accelerated return. S&C: the load-management argument for protecting a repaired meniscus long-term.

**CB3 - Rotator cuff in the overhead athlete** *(Day 1, 14:26, 20-min floor debate)* -- 27-year-old national-circuit competitive tennis player (singles, All India Tennis Association ranking points, trains 5x/week), 8-month history of deep posterosuperior shoulder pain in the late-cocking/deceleration phase of the serve, missed the last two ranking tournaments. Exam: positive posterosuperior internal-impingement sign and relocation test, GIRD ~25&deg; versus the non-dominant side, scapular dyskinesis on the wall push-up test, intact anterior stability (negative apprehension) and an intact biceps-labral complex (negative O'Brien's/biceps-load, ruling out the SLAP-dominant pathology CB12 covers). Four months of generic subacromial-strengthening physiotherapy -- not internal-impingement-specific, no posterior-capsule or kinetic-chain-directed protocol -- gave partial relief only. MR arthrogram: Ellman grade III (&gt;50% thickness) articular-sided partial-thickness supraspinatus tear (a PASTA lesion) with mild posterosuperior labral fraying but an intact biceps anchor; muscle bulk preserved, no fatty infiltration. Surgical debate: a properly-run internal-impingement-specific conservative programme first -- the prior physio trial wasn't the right one, and return-to-sport after internal-impingement surgery is inconsistent (25-94% across procedures) -- vs arthroscopic debridement without formal repair, still the commonly taught default for these lesions, sparing a young reparable shoulder the stiffness/retear risk of a repair construct -- vs arthroscopic repair (transtendon technique preferred, preserving the intact bursal layer), on 2025 evidence that debridement alone underperforms repair even at this grade, with genuine equipoise between transtendon and tear-completion-and-repair technique once repair is the choice. Physio: GIRD-directed posterior-capsule stretching (sleeper vs cross-body stretch -- equally effective, so patient tolerance and adherence decide) and scapular/kinetic-chain retraining, criteria- not calendar-based return to serve-specific loading. S&C: closing the core-endurance and scapular-stabiliser deficit that drove the overload in the first place, velocity-based return-to-serve progression, not a fixed number of weeks.

*Correction (Aug 2026, second pass): the case, tear pattern, and surgical debate were rewritten from scratch after two prior patches were rejected. The masked-subscapularis version (repair vs repair+biceps tenodesis vs latissimus dorsi/pectoralis major transfer) was internally consistent but wrong for this block on two counts. First, anatomy: subscapularis tears are a hyperextension/lift-off or traumatic-dislocation pathology, not what repetitive overhead loading produces -- GIRD-driven posterosuperior humeral-head contact wears the articular side of supraspinatus (and sometimes infraspinatus), not subscapularis, so "overhead athlete" was a label attached to the wrong tendon. Second, dramaturgy: "repair vs transfer for the irreparable tear" is a severity-triage algorithm read off one MRI (what do you do if it turns out reparable vs if it doesn't), not three surgeons genuinely disputing the same patient -- and it thematically duplicated CB11's transfer-salvage flavour. The corrected case is the single most common cuff lesion this audience's racquet-sport and throwing athletes actually present with (a high-grade PASTA lesion from internal impingement), and the three positions are a live, current, unresolved three-way split on the same MRI, not a triage tree: Challoumas et al.'s 2026 systematic review of surgical management of internal impingement in overhead athletes found return-to-sport "variable" (25-94% across procedures, debridement of articular partial cuff tears 50-85%) and explicitly recommends patient-specific decision-making after conservative care fails, not a default surgical answer (*Clin Shoulder Elb* 2026;29(1):115-133, DOI: [10.5397/cise.2025.01123](https://doi.org/10.5397/cise.2025.01123)); Papadopoulos et al.'s 2025 comparative study found arthroscopic repair beat debridement on VAS, Constant-Murley and ASES scores even at Ellman grade II -- explicitly framed as challenging "commonly accepted practice" (*JSES Rev Rep Tech* 2025;5(4):583-588, DOI: [10.1016/j.xrrt.2025.05.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xrrt.2025.05.008)); and Thamrongskulsiri et al.'s 2023 meta-analysis (7 studies, 497 shoulders, DOI: [10.1007/s00167-023-07502-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00167-023-07502-z)) together with a 2026 prospective cohort out of Pune (Patil & Bakshi, *Ann Afr Med*, DOI: [10.4103/aam.aam_29_26](https://doi.org/10.4103/aam.aam_29_26)) both found genuine equipoise between transtendon and tear-completion repair technique -- "the optimal repair technique remains uncertain," per the Pune paper. Physio evidence (sleeper vs cross-body stretch for GIRD, equally effective): de Araújo et al. 2026, *J Shoulder Elbow Surg*, DOI: [10.1016/j.jse.2025.12.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jse.2025.12.017); foundational RCT, Chepeha et al. 2018, *Clin J Sport Med*, DOI: [10.1097/JSM.0000000000000434](https://doi.org/10.1097/JSM.0000000000000434). S&C evidence (core-endurance and scapular-stabiliser deficit in athletes with scapular dyskinesis): Tahani et al. 2026, *Sci Rep*, DOI: [10.1038/s41598-026-56082-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56082-8). Imaging/pathophysiology reference for internal impingement, GIRD and PSI: Goes et al. 2023, *Radiographics*, DOI: [10.1148/rg.230094](https://doi.org/10.1148/rg.230094). By contrast, a generic degenerative full-thickness posterosuperior tear in an active-but-not-specifically-overhead patient was considered and set aside: Moosmayer et al.'s 15-year RCT follow-up (*J Bone Joint Surg Am* 2024;106(19):1785-1796, DOI: [10.2106/JBJS.24.00065](https://doi.org/10.2106/JBJS.24.00065)) shows repair pulling durably ahead of physiotherapy for small-medium tears at long-term follow-up -- that axis is trending toward resolution, not the "genuinely live, unresolved" controversy this block needs. Distinct from CB11 (58-year-old, traumatic fall, massive full-thickness tear with fatty infiltration, a salvage-vs-arthroplasty decision) on every axis -- age, mechanism, tear depth, muscle quality, decision -- and from CB12 (SLAP-dominant pathology, explicitly intact cuff) by keeping the biceps-labral complex intact here.*

*Correction (Aug 2026, third pass): the Scientific Committee rejected the second-pass version above with the objection, verbatim, "we feel in overhead athlete u dont get rotator cuff tears." That claim doesn't hold as a blanket statement -- imaging series in exactly this population show the opposite. In asymptomatic elite overhead athletes (a cohort that included tennis alongside baseball), 40% of dominant shoulders showed MRI evidence of partial- or full-thickness rotator cuff tears versus 0% of non-dominant shoulders, and none developed symptoms over 5 years' follow-up (Connor et al., *Am J Sports Med* 2003;31(5):724-7, DOI: [10.1177/03635465030310051501](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465030310051501)); in asymptomatic professional pitchers specifically, the labrum was abnormal in 79% of shoulders (Miniaci et al., *Am J Sports Med* 2002;30(1):66-73, DOI: [10.1177/03635465020300012501](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465020300012501)). Structural rotator cuff findings, including articular-sided partial tears, are not rare or anomalous in overhead athletes -- they are the well-described downstream finding of the internal-impingement mechanism this case already describes, and the Challoumas 2026 review cited above (42 studies, 1,420 overhead athletes, mean age 27.3) exists precisely because surgeons operate on this lesion in this population often enough to generate that evidence base. The committee's blanket claim was the wrong reason to reject the case.

The real defect was the sport, not the pathology. Badminton has no direct published evidence of rotator-cuff involvement -- the badminton literature that exists is limited to general shoulder-muscle-synergy and forearm-tendon-loading biomechanics work (Xiao et al. 2026, *Front Bioeng Biotechnol*, DOI: [10.3389/fbioe.2026.1780880](https://doi.org/10.3389/fbioe.2026.1780880); Tajik et al. 2025, *Front Sports Act Living*, DOI: [10.3389/fspor.2025.1596670](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2025.1596670)), and both describe the forehand smash as a ~3ms, forearm-pronation/wrist-extensor-dominant impact rather than the sustained late-cocking, maximal-external-rotation position that actually drives internal impingement -- a real biomechanical gap, not just an evidence gap. Tennis is the better-evidenced racquet sport for this exact case: professional tour players show glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD) in 87.4% of dominant shoulders (vs 56.3% non-dominant) across 270 shoulders assessed in-season, a pattern the authors term "tennis shoulder syndrome" (López-Vidriero Tejedor et al. 2023, *Knee Surg Sports Traumatol Arthrosc* 31(6):2152-9, DOI: [10.1007/s00167-023-07310-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00167-023-07310-5)); elite female tour players show infraspinatus atrophy in 52% of dominant shoulders, common and usually asymptomatic but a marker of chronic posterior-cuff overload (Young et al. 2015, *Am J Sports Med* 43(8):1989-93, DOI: [10.1177/0363546515588177](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546515588177)); and tennis players with clinical impingement show measurably different scapular kinematics at maximal external rotation during the serve versus those without it (Kim et al. 2023, *J Back Musculoskelet Rehabil* 36(3):685-90, DOI: [10.3233/BMR-220096](https://doi.org/10.3233/BMR-220096)). The case above is rewritten from a badminton mixed-doubles player to a 27-year-old national-circuit competitive singles player for exactly this reason -- same pathology, same MRI findings, same three-way surgical debate, sport swapped to the one with an actual evidence base behind it. This also keeps Day 1's joint/sport variety intact -- CB1 (fast bowler/thrower) already covers cricket-style throwing and CB2 already covers kabaddi, so a throwing-sport swap for CB3 would have duplicated one of those populations rather than fixing the actual problem.*

**CB5 - Hamstring tear & reconditioning** *(Day 2, 08:30)* -- 26-year-old track-and-field hurdler, proximal hamstring tendon avulsion (2 of 3 tendons, >2cm retraction) clearing a hurdle, failed an initial conservative trial. *Age, injury grade and sport corrected (Aug 2026) -- age was unspecified (age is the single strongest hamstring-strain risk factor, and the surgical cohorts this case needs run mid-20s); the original "grade 2b" tear is a musculotendinous-junction injury that's conservative-management territory, not a surgical one, so it's now a 2-3-tendon proximal free avulsion with meaningful retraction, matching the literature's actual surgical threshold; and "sprinter/footballer" was dropped because straight-line sprinting and football produce musculotendinous strains, not free-tendon avulsions -- the avulsion mechanism this case needs is forced hip flexion with knee extension, the specific loading pattern of hurdling (and water-skiing), which is why hurdler replaces them.* Surgical/sports-physician debate: conservative vs surgical repair thresholds at this retraction/tendon-count level, MRI's real value for "Monday's decision." Physio: L-protocol vs conventional rehab timelines, lengthened-state training. S&C: sprint/hurdle-clearance reintroduction dosing and the reinjury-risk argument for not rushing return-to-hurdling.

**CB14 - Distal biceps tendon rupture** -- genuinely new territory, nothing like it on the current grid. 32-year-old competitive powerlifter, felt a pop with sharp antecubital pain during a max-effort deadlift, "Popeye" deformity, positive hook test, MRI confirms complete rupture with up to 2cm retraction, presents on day 12. *Timing/retraction tightened (Aug 2026) from day 18/2-3cm -- direct, non-augmented repair (all three options below) becomes technically difficult beyond ~4 weeks or with retraction >3cm, at which point most surgeons shift toward graft reconstruction, a technique none of the three options represent; day 12/&le;2cm keeps this a clean primary-repair-technique debate rather than a "should this even be primary repair" tangent.* Nonoperative management is not a credible surgeon position here -- meaningful supination-strength/work-output deficits (17-37%) persist without repair, unacceptable for an athlete who needs full pulling strength -- so all three surgeons operate and disagree only on technique/timing: single-incision cortical button repair (best supination-strength/endurance data, argues delay past 4 weeks risks needing graft augmentation) vs double-incision transosseous fixation (historic gold standard, lower reported PIN-injury risk, trades this for higher heterotopic-ossification/synostosis risk and a second scar) vs suture-anchor fixation (biomechanically comparable to a button, easier revision if it fails). **CB16 - Achilles tendon rupture** *(added Aug 2026 -- the largest single gap found in the slate-level review; a high-volume, career-threatening injury with no block anywhere in the programme)* -- 34-year-old competitive club badminton player, push-off for a backhand lunge, audible pop and the classic "felt kicked from behind," presents day 6. Palpable 4cm gap, positive Thompson test, resting plantarflexion asymmetry; ultrasound confirms full-thickness rupture 4cm from the insertion with a 12mm gap in equinus. Surgical debate, and it is a genuinely three-cornered one: non-operative management with accelerated functional rehab (identical 12-month function, avoids infection and nerve injury entirely) vs open repair (roughly ten-fold reduction in re-rupture -- the argument being that an athlete who must push off cannot absorb a ~6% career-ending risk, plus tendon-elongation and plantarflexion-strength loss) vs percutaneous/minimally-invasive repair (re-rupture equal to open, infection near-zero, priced in sural nerve palsy). The 2022 *NEJM* three-arm randomised trial of 554 patients is the spine of this block: no difference in patient-reported outcome across all three arms, but re-rupture 6.2% non-operative vs 0.6% open vs 0.6% MIS, and 5.2% nerve injury in the MIS arm (Myhrvold et al., DOI: [10.1056/NEJMoa2108447](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2108447)); see also Ochen et al., *BMJ* 2019 (surgery cuts re-rupture but adds complications, DOI: [10.1136/bmj.k5120](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.k5120)) and Attia et al., *AJSM* 2021 (DOI: [10.1177/03635465211053619](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465211053619)). Physio: functional bracing protocol, heel-wedge weaning, monitoring tendon elongation via heel-rise height, ATRS milestones -- and the honest finding that accelerated versus delayed rehab showed no outcome difference (Coopmans et al., *J Foot Ankle Surg* 2022, DOI: [10.1053/j.jfas.2021.07.007](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.jfas.2021.07.007)), so what actually drives recovery? S&C: the persistent plantarflexor deficit that outlasts every protocol, heel-rise endurance and reactive-strength criteria before return to court. **Floor hook:** *"Identical function, ten-fold re-rupture difference, zero difference in patient-reported score. Who owns that decision -- and does your answer change at day 6 versus day 21?"* *Badminton is the deliberate and evidence-backed sport choice here: in a 140-case badminton injury series Achilles rupture was the second commonest diagnosis at 13.9% (Saragaglia et al., DOI: [10.1007/s00590-022-03372-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00590-022-03372-2)) -- this is also the block that brings India's second sport into the programme, and the only case that lands in the 30-55 age band the scheduled nine leave empty. Note for chairs: **UKSTAR (Costa et al., Lancet 2020) is NOT an operative-versus-non-operative trial** -- it randomised patients already assigned to non-operative care between plaster cast and functional brace. If a panellist cites it as the op-vs-non-op evidence, correct it.* Keep insertional/mid-portion Achilles **tendinopathy** out of this block -- it is a separate, higher-volume Indian problem with its own three-way (load-based rehab vs debridement + speedbridge vs FHL transfer) and would make a reserve block of its own rather than being absorbed here.

**CB17 - Patellofemoral instability -- when do you add bone?** *(added Aug 2026 -- fills the knee under-weighting and, with CB2, gives the programme its second female athlete)* -- 17-year-old female state-level volleyball middle-blocker, third lateral patellar dislocation, this one non-contact on landing. No weight-bearing CT assumed. **Genuinely borderline:** clinical tests cannot separate stable from unstable injuries, and a deltoid injury combined with a partial syndesmotic tear becomes unstable in cadaveric testing. *Settled, so not debated: stable injuries do well without surgery and frank diastasis needs stabilisation (Calder et al., *Arthroscopy* 2016, DOI: [10.1016/j.arthro.2015.10.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.arthro.2015.10.003)); suture-button versus screw is largely settled in favour of suture-button (meta-analysis of 16 RCTs, mostly fracture patients -- Cehic et al., *JBI Evid Synth* 2026, DOI: [10.11124/JBIES-24-00547](https://doi.org/10.11124/JBIES-24-00547)) -- do not build this debate on the fixation device.* Surgical debate: "don't operate on a scan" -- boot, protected weight-bearing, criteria-based rehab with planned re-examination (return to sport ~98% for both suture-button and non-operative care in an elite-athlete meta-analysis -- Li et al., *J Orthop Surg Res* 2025, DOI: [10.1186/s13018-025-06566-6](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-025-06566-6); grade II injuries were equally stable on external-torque CT at more than 2 years with or without surgery -- Beeler et al., *J Foot Ankle Surg* 2026, DOI: [10.1053/j.jfas.2026.05.013](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.jfas.2026.05.013)) vs "arthroscopy is the test" -- arthroscopy this week and suture-button only if sagittal fibular translation is 2mm or more (77.5% sensitivity, 88.9% specificity -- Lubberts et al., *Foot Ankle Int* 2019, DOI: [10.1177/1071100719879673](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100719879673)), leaving the deltoid alone (professional footballers stabilised for instability reached first match at a mean 103 days -- D'Hooghe et al., *BJSM* 2020, DOI: [10.1136/bjsports-2018-100298](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-100298)) vs "rebuild it" -- suture-button plus AITFL augmentation plus deltoid repair (combined repair restored rotational stability where isolated syndesmotic or isolated deltoid repair did not -- Bachmaier et al., *AJSM* 2025, DOI: [10.1177/03635465251361148](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465251361148); the support is cadaveric, which the floor should press). Physio: the dorsiflexion lunge as the tracking test; stable non-operative injuries returned at 45 days versus 64 after stabilisation (Calder 2016); caution with early dorsiflexion and external-rotation loading after deltoid repair. S&C: straight-line running, then deceleration and cutting, contact last; watch for fibular pain under load after suture-button fixation (peri-implant fibular fractures reported in elite athletes -- Hong et al., *OJSM* 2023, DOI: [10.1177/23259671231206185](https://doi.org/10.1177/23259671231206185)). **Floor hook:** *"If the non-operative and suture-button groups return to sport at the same rate, what justifies rebuilding a deltoid that isn't fully torn in an athlete with no fracture?"* *Overlap control: external-rotation mechanism with the ATFL intact on MRI -- the lateral-ligament debate (CB13) and chronic instability (CB6) are ruled out of order. For Indian settings without weight-bearing CT, dynamic ultrasound is a possible low-cost alternative, but only cadaveric data support it so far (Hagemeijer et al., *KSSTA* 2022, DOI: [10.1007/s00167-022-07058-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00167-022-07058-4)).*

**CB20 - The subtle Lisfranc: fix, fuse or flex?** *(added Sep 2026 -- benchmark gap: "Back in the Game" gave Lisfranc a whole themed session)* -- 21-year-old female state-level basketball player; landed from a rebound on an opponent's foot -- axial load on a plantarflexed foot. Basketball and a female athlete by evidence: an 82-athlete series of unstable ligamentous Lisfranc injuries had a mean age of 21 and included 18 women and 13 basketball players (Porter et al., *Foot Ankle Int* 2018, DOI: [10.1177/1071100718802264](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100718802264)). Exam: plantar midfoot ecchymosis, painful piano-key test at the second ray. Imaging: non-weight-bearing radiographs normal; bilateral standing radiographs show the first cuneiform to second metatarsal interval 2.5mm wider than the uninjured side; MRI -- plantar Lisfranc ligament torn, first-to-second intercuneiform ligament injured, no fracture. **Genuinely borderline:** low-energy, purely ligamentous and subtly unstable -- each school can claim her. *Settled, so not debated: stable undisplaced injuries do well non-operatively (by consensus rather than a powered trial -- Ponkilainen et al. 2024, DOI: [10.1177/14574969241295585](https://doi.org/10.1177/14574969241295585)); anatomic reduction matters (residual displacement over 2mm predicted worse professional careers -- McHale et al., *AJSM* 2017, DOI: [10.1177/0363546517697297](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546517697297)).* Surgical debate: rigid fixation with planned hardware removal -- transarticular screws or dorsal bridge plating (the established athlete pathway, return to sport at 7.5 +/- 2.1 months in Porter 2018; a 10-year trial found bridge plating held up against fusion of the first tarsometatarsal joint -- Grün et al., *JBJS* 2026, DOI: [10.2106/JBJS.26.00020](https://doi.org/10.2106/JBJS.26.00020); weakness: radiographic arthritis in 11 of 24 bridge-plated first tarsometatarsal joints -- Stødle et al., *Foot Ankle Int* 2020, DOI: [10.1177/1071100720925815](https://doi.org/10.1177/1071100720925815)) vs primary partial arthrodesis of the medial two or three tarsometatarsal joints (the only RCT in purely ligamentous injuries favoured fusion: midfoot score 88 vs 68.6, patient-rated activity 92% vs 65% of pre-injury -- Ly & Coetzee, *JBJS* 2006, DOI: [10.2106/JBJS.E.00228](https://doi.org/10.2106/JBJS.E.00228); no second operation; weakness: no trial in athletes, and a newer underpowered RCT found no difference -- Ponkilainen et al., *Foot Ankle Int* 2024, DOI: [10.1177/10711007241232667](https://doi.org/10.1177/10711007241232667)) vs flexible suture-button or suture-tape fixation (elite athletes returned at 12-16 weeks -- Sullivan et al., *OJSM* 2022, DOI: [10.1177/23259671221102969](https://doi.org/10.1177/23259671221102969); cadaveric stability comparable to transarticular screws -- Koroneos et al., *AJSM* 2022, DOI: [10.1177/03635465221118580](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465221118580); weakness: case-series evidence only, and early-recovery advantages largely fade by one year -- Cheng et al., *Foot* 2026, DOI: [10.1016/j.foot.2026.102278](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foot.2026.102278)). *"Fix or fuse" is explicitly live: the JBJS 2026 commentary is titled "Fix or Fuse: The Lisfranc Debate Persists" (DOI: [10.2106/JBJS.26.00749](https://doi.org/10.2106/JBJS.26.00749)).* Physio: weight-bearing timelines differ completely by pathway (full weight-bearing at 4 weeks after suture-button fixation in the Sullivan series, versus typically about 6 weeks non-weight-bearing after screws or fusion); intrinsic foot and calf capacity, single-leg calf-raise and hop symmetry. S&C: landing and cutting mechanics, footwear stiffness, rebuilding jump volume, and what a fused medial column means for force transfer. **Floor hook:** *"With no powered trial in athletes, should a 21-year-old be offered a fusion that removes the chance of a second operation, or a motion-preserving repair that removes the certainty?"* **Folded in here -- turf toe (researched and rejected as a standalone block):** fewer than 2% of cases need surgery and the surgical evidence is small case series, so there is no three-way surgical debate. Use it as a floor coda: *"Which forefoot and midfoot injuries in a jumping athlete must have weight-bearing or stress imaging before a return-to-play decision?"* (Gupta et al., *Curr Rev Musculoskelet Med* 2023, DOI: [10.1007/s12178-023-09870-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12178-023-09870-y)). *Overlap control: strictly midfoot -- no ankle instability (CB6, CB13), no syndesmosis (CB19), no Achilles (CB16). CB6 is also basketball; the female patient and midfoot pathology keep them distinct, but do not schedule them adjacent. The Grün 2026 figures come from the journal commentary -- verify against the full text before quoting.*

**CB21 - The focal cartilage defect in the young footballer** *(added Sep 2026 -- benchmark gap: "Back in the Game" ran four cartilage/osteochondral cases plus an orthobiologics talk; also flagged in the August slate review)* -- 24-year-old male semi-professional footballer; non-contact twist while cutting 8 months ago, knee swollen but stable; since then medial pain and effusion after matches; no previous surgery; 4 months of supervised rehab failed and he cannot play. Football and male by evidence: cartilage lesions in footballers are predominantly femoral condylar, mean age 25.7 (Andrade et al., *Arthroscopy* 2016, DOI: [10.1016/j.arthro.2016.01.055](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.arthro.2016.01.055)); FIFA, ICRS and Aspetar published a two-part consensus on exactly this problem (Kon et al., *BJSM* 2025, DOI: [10.1136/bjsports-2024-108960](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2024-108960); Papakostas et al., *BJSM* 2026, DOI: [10.1136/bjsports-2025-110676](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2025-110676)). Exam: effusion, tender medial femoral condyle in flexion, ligaments stable, meniscal tests negative, quadriceps wasting. 3T MRI: 18 x 14mm (about 2.5cm2) full-thickness (ICRS grade 4) defect of the central weight-bearing medial femoral condyle, well-shouldered, subchondral plate intact, mild underlying bone oedema, no loose body; ACL and both menisci intact. Standing long-leg radiograph: neutral alignment, so unloading osteotomy is not indicated. **At 2.5cm2 all three positions below are legitimate -- a clash of schools, not a size-based triage.** *Adult traumatic defect chosen deliberately over juvenile OCD, which is a stability-and-fixation debate CB10 already runs. Plain microfracture is effectively consensus-obsolete for footballers (rated inappropriate or uncertain in the football consensus), so no position defends it.* Surgical debate: single-stage osteochondral autograft transfer (hyaline cartilage and the fastest return; in athletes 93% returned to their previous level versus 52% after microfracture -- Gudas et al., *Arthroscopy* 2005, DOI: [10.1016/j.arthro.2005.06.018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.arthro.2005.06.018); failure 14% versus 38% at 10 years -- Gudas et al., *AJSM* 2012, DOI: [10.1177/0363546512458763](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546512458763); quickest return, about 6.6 months, in a 2025 meta-analysis -- Kunze et al., *AJSM* 2025, DOI: [10.1177/03635465241280975](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465241280975); weakness: 2.5cm2 needs several plugs, so donor-site pain and surface congruity) vs two-stage cell-based cartilage repair (better knee function at 10 years with similar failure in an RCT -- Snow et al., *Osteoarthritis Cartilage* 2026, DOI: [10.1016/j.joca.2026.03.112](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joca.2026.03.112), though in patients who had failed a prior treatment; favoured in the football consensus for players prioritising long-term outcome; weakness: two operations, cost, a longer return) vs single-stage bone-marrow stimulation augmented with a cell-free scaffold, with or without bone marrow concentrate (the affordability and single-operation argument -- Reddy et al., *BMJ Open Sport Exerc Med* 2026, DOI: [10.1136/bmjsem-2025-002889](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjsem-2025-002889), low-certainty evidence; this surgeon must concede the consensus rating of marrow stimulation). **India reality check, correcting an earlier assumption:** cell-based cartilage repair *is* available in India -- a product with Indian regulatory marketing authorisation, in use since 2008, with a near-10-year Indian series (Pardiwala et al., *Knee* 2024, DOI: [10.1016/j.knee.2024.05.006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.knee.2024.05.006)); cost, not availability, is the barrier. Fresh osteochondral allograft is *not* a realistic Indian position (complex logistics, no Indian clinical series found -- DOI: [10.1007/s43465-022-00661-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43465-022-00661-0)) and is deliberately excluded. Physio: each construct loads differently -- press-fit plugs, maturing cultured cells, a scaffold; effusion-guided loading, quadriceps recovery, criteria- not time-based progression. S&C: impact tolerance for high-speed running, deceleration and cutting; graded return to impact and match-load monitoring. **Floor hook:** *"Does a scaffold-augmented marrow stimulation today burn the bridge to cell-based repair tomorrow?"* -- one series found cell-based repair failed three times as often after prior marrow stimulation (Minas et al., *AJSM* 2009, DOI: [10.1177/0363546508330137](https://doi.org/10.1177/0363546508330137)), while the 2026 RCT found prior marrow stimulation made no difference (Snow 2026); in India the cheaper first operation is the default, so this matters. *Overlap control: CB4 is 56, varus and degenerative -- this patient is 24, neutral, menisci intact, osteotomy excluded; CB10 is adolescent OCD stability; ACL intact (not CB2); medial femoral condyle, not patellofemoral (not CB17). The Indian cell-product literature names the brand -- use generic terms on slides. Two figures (scaffold versus microfracture; fastest return after autograft transfer) come from abstracts -- verify against full text before quoting.*

**CB22 - Grade III AC joint dislocation: IIIA or IIIB?** *(added Sep 2026 -- benchmark gap: "Back in the Game" ran a high-grade AC dislocation; also flagged in the August slate review)* -- 24-year-old male state-level road cyclist; crash onto the point of the shoulder with the arm adducted; seen at day 10. Cycling, male and age by evidence: in a Danish cohort 51 of 106 AC dislocations came from cycling, with age peaking at 20-24 and a male-to-female ratio of 8.6:1 (Haugaard et al., *Shoulder Elbow* 2022, DOI: [10.1177/17585732221123314](https://doi.org/10.1177/17585732221123314)); bicycles were also the leading cause in US emergency departments (Boufadel et al., *Shoulder Elbow* 2025, DOI: [10.1177/17585732251320015](https://doi.org/10.1177/17585732251320015)) -- the evidence is Danish and American, not Indian. Exam: prominent reducible distal clavicle, tender AC and coracoclavicular ligaments, pain on cross-body adduction, neurovascular examination normal. Imaging: bilateral Zanca views show the coracoclavicular distance about 80% greater than the uninjured side (grade III); the axillary view excludes grade IV; the cross-body adduction view shows the clavicle overriding the acromion. **Why grade III, and why day 10:** it is where the debate is genuinely open. The ISAKOS consensus split grade III into IIIA (stable) and IIIB (clavicle overriding on the cross-body view with scapular dysfunction refractory to rehab) (Beitzel et al., *Arthroscopy* 2014, DOI: [10.1016/j.arthro.2013.11.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.arthro.2013.11.005)). This patient has the radiographic sign of IIIB, but at day 10 nobody can yet say whether the scapular dysfunction will settle. Surgical debate: non-operative first, surgery only if it fails (a meta-analysis of five RCTs, 367 patients, found no Constant-score difference at 2 years or more; early scores favoured non-operative care and surgery carried more minor complications -- Lameire et al., *OJSM* 2026, DOI: [10.1177/23259671261440812](https://doi.org/10.1177/23259671261440812)) vs early arthroscopic coracoclavicular stabilisation with suture-button fixation (the "no difference" finding is fragile: in three of seven RCTs, losses to follow-up exceeded the number of outcome changes needed to flip the result, and those trials averaged age 39 and were 92% male -- Althoff et al., *Orthopedics* 2026, DOI: [10.3928/01477447-20260611-01](https://doi.org/10.3928/01477447-20260611-01); a younger RCT, mean age 31, found equal DASH but better UCLA scores and cosmetic satisfaction after surgery -- Lara et al., *AJSM* 2026, DOI: [10.1177/03635465251395220](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465251395220); return to play after surgery for grade III was 98.7% at about 4.9 months -- Cleary et al., *AJSM* 2023, DOI: [10.1177/03635465231178784](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465231178784)) vs two-plane stabilisation -- coracoclavicular plus acromioclavicular augmentation, because an overriding clavicle is horizontal instability that vertical-only fixation leaves untreated (coracoclavicular distance held better with augmentation in a 70-patient RCT -- Ambrožič et al., *J Clin Med* 2025, DOI: [10.3390/jcm14093161](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm14093161) -- but with no clinical difference at one year, which this surgeon must answer). *Chair's note: Lara 2026 used a modified Weaver-Dunn, a non-anatomic reconstruction -- which weakens the second surgeon's reliance on it.* Physio: examining scapular dyskinesis and cross-body instability in a painful shoulder; what rehab must demonstrate, and by when, before calling it IIIA or IIIB; sling duration and early range of motion. S&C: handlebar and aero-position loading, early indoor-trainer riding, upward-rotator strengthening, re-crash risk (practice guidance -- no cycling-specific trials found). **Floor hook:** *"The 2026 meta-analyses pool the same trials of 39-year-olds. Is a trial of rehab for this 24-year-old evidence-based, or does it waste the window for primary fixation?"* *Overlap control: no pathology overlap with CB1, CB3, CB11 or CB12 -- but this is the fifth shoulder-region block (see Section 7). Low-cost hook-plate fixation is still common in Indian practice; raise it from the floor against suture-button fixation (Lim et al., *JSES Rev Rep Tech* 2025, DOI: [10.1016/j.xrrt.2025.04.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xrrt.2025.04.002)).*

## 4. Hall B — Rehabilitation, Physiotherapy, S&C & Return to Play

**Format:** 12–15 min talks; blocks close with a moderated discussion rather than per-talk Q&A. Two ticketed, capped hands-on workshops.

**B1 · Criteria-based rehab & the RTP decision** — "time is not a criterion"; what proportion of athletes would actually pass a full battery at 9 months post-ACLR; return to participation → sport → performance; psychological readiness (ACL-RSI) and the athlete whose knee passes but whose head doesn't; who owns the decision and how it's documented. Closes by building a one-page RTP criteria sheet live from the floor.
**Debate:** *"No athlete should be cleared for pivoting sport before 9 months post-ACLR."*

**B2 · Objective testing, screening & technology** — force plates in a small clinic: the five CMJ metrics worth reporting and the twenty that aren't; handheld dynamometry done properly (belt fixation, normalisation, MDC); isokinetics vs field testing in 2027; movement screening after FMS; markerless motion capture — good enough to change a decision?; **panel:** *"What would you buy first with ₹5 lakh?"*

**B3 · Tendinopathy & load-driven pain** — tendon pain as a loading problem; isometrics for analgesia (where "45 seconds × 5" came from and when it fails); heavy slow resistance vs eccentrics for patellar tendinopathy; insertional vs mid-portion Achilles; gluteal tendinopathy — compression is the enemy; energy storage and release, the phase everyone skips.

**W1 · Hands-on carousel** (90 min, three 28-min stations, ~30 per station):
1. **Objective testing bench** — live CMJ/drop jump on volunteers, reading force–time curves on screen; dynamometry with vs without belt fixation (showing the tester-strength artefact); writing the actual report line a surgeon and a coach will each read.
2. **Tendon loading lab** — Spanish squat rigged with a belt and pole (no proprietary kit); HSR tempo work; modifying every exercise for a clinic with one bench, one step, one barbell; the 24-hour pain-response rule on case vignettes.
3. **Plyometric progression** — pogo → hop → bound ladder with landing-quality criteria; take a 3-month post-ACLR athlete to month 7 with only cones, a box and floor space.

**W2 · Field-based RTP testing & hamstring reconditioning** (Day 2, 90 min) — build a 40-minute RTP battery with no lab (505, hop series, Askling H-test, sprint exposure); hamstring L-protocol and Nordic technique coached hands-on; sprint reintroduction dosing; the pass/fail sheet you hand the coach.

**B5 · Muscle injury & the hamstring** — classifying injury (BAMIC, MLG-R) and whether MRI changes Monday; L-protocol vs conventional (28 vs 51 days) and reinjury; lengthened-state training and adherence reality; calf and adductor — the two you're under-rehabbing; return to running → return to sprinting.

**B6 · Load management & injury risk** — what happened to ACWR (from "predicts injury" to a cluster RCT in 482 elite youth footballers showing no difference); metrics that survive a real Indian club season; bowling workload and the fast bowler's back; sleep, energy availability and travel — the load that never appears in the GPS file.

**B7 · The athlete who isn't a 25-year-old male** — youth resistance training is protective (and how to say that to a parent); apophyseal injury — load modification, not rest; the female athlete and menstrual cycle: what low-quality evidence permits you to claim; REDs as a modifiable rehab variable; para-athlete rehab and why your normative data doesn't apply.

**B4 · Adjuncts under scrutiny** *(reserve)* — BFR: what 2024–25 meta-analyses actually show (comparable, not superior) and the three groups where the cuff earns its place; manual therapy as a short-half-life adjunct; shockwave, dry needling and the physio's filter for injectable-adjacent claims.

## 5. Hall B, science stream — Sports Science, Nutrition & Athlete Health

**This is not a separate hall.** These blocks run inside Hall B, and the strongest of them (concussion, sports cardiology, anti-doping) are promoted to plenary session so the surgical audience gets them too — which is the point of collapsing the third room rather than deleting it. The `C` numbering is kept only so the committee can match sessions against the earlier three-hall draft.

**Format:** 12-min talks, 4 per hour + chaired panel. `[C]` = has a formal consensus basis — cite the document on the slide (useful for the CME file). `[IN]` = India-specific angle.

**C1 · Energy availability & REDs** `[C: IOC 2023 REDs consensus]` — what actually changed from RED-S to REDs; applying the CAT2 traffic-light stratification across a 30-athlete squad; screening for low energy availability without a DXA in an Indian academy `[IN]`; male REDs — the missed diagnosis in wrestling, boxing and weightlifting `[IN]`.

**C2 · Nutrition periodisation on Indian diets** `[IN]` — fuel for the work required without inducing LEA; hitting 1.6–2.2 g/kg protein on a vegetarian diet (dal, dairy, the leucine threshold problem); building a pre-competition menu from rice, roti, poha and idli rather than translating a Western one; feeding the residential hostel athlete — what a SAI/Khelo India mess can and cannot deliver.

**C3 · Hydration & thermoregulation in Indian heat** `[C: ACSM 2023]` `[IN]` — WBGT is not air temperature: writing a heat policy a match referee will enforce; sweat rate and sodium at 33°C/85% RH and why imported prescriptions under-deliver; exertional heat stroke — cool first, transport second (cold-water immersion to <39°C before the ambulance moves); ten-day acclimatisation for travelling teams.

**C4 · Supplements & ergogenic aids** `[C: WADA strict liability]` — the five that survive scrutiny (creatine, caffeine, beta-alanine, nitrate, bicarbonate); contamination risk in the Indian supplement market and what batch certification does and doesn't cover `[IN]`; strict liability explained to an athlete; ayurvedic and herbal ergogenics — the evidence-to-marketing gap `[IN]`.

**C8 · Exercise physiology & performance testing** — durability, the endurance parameter your VO₂max test misses; critical power/speed as a field tool; lactate thresholds without a lab; force plates and the CMJ — which metrics track fatigue and which track noise.

**C9 · Wearables, AI & athlete monitoring** — only ~43% of wearable studies report adequate validity: a buyer's checklist before your federation signs; GPS and workload after a decade of criticism; why AI injury-prediction models keep failing external validation; **athlete data governance under India's DPDP Act** — consent, ownership and the federation contract `[IN]`.

**C12 · Concussion** `[C: Amsterdam 2022 / 6th International Consensus]` — SCAT6 and SCOAT6 in practice; the new visible signs mandating immediate removal (tonic posturing, impact seizure, ataxia, blank look); a Child SCAT6 protocol an Indian school can run without a physician on site `[IN]`.

**C13 · Anti-doping & WADA 2026 updates** `[C: 2026 Prohibited List, in force 1 Jan 2026]` — the 2026 List decoded for team clinicians (carbon monoxide as new M1.4, cell components, revised salmeterol intervals); building a TUE file that gets approved; **India top of WADA's ADRV table three years running** — 7,113 tests, 260 AAFs, 3.6% positivity, read honestly; where India's positives cluster (athletics 76, weightlifting 43, wrestling 29) and what support staff can change `[IN]`.

## 6. What makes this programme distinctly Indian

A translated Western programme is the failure mode. These threads give SIC 2027 an identity:

- **Kabaddi** — Indian series data show ACL tears in 89.5% of injured players, 68.4% with meniscal involvement, 57.9% retiring from the sport, 16.6 months mean time lost. That single dataset earns a case in the ACL, meniscus, hip/groin *and* rehab sessions.
- **Cricket bowling workload** and the fast bowler's back — across surgical, rehab and load-management halls.
- **Heat and humidity** — heat policy, sweat sodium, acclimatisation. Non-negotiable for an Indian audience.
- **Vegetarian athlete nutrition** — protein targets and the leucine threshold on Indian diets.
- **Doping** — India has led WADA's violation table three years running. Confronting it directly is the most valuable thing the science stream can do — and as a plenary session it now reaches the surgeons too.
- **Rehab without a lab** — most Indian clinics have no isokinetic dynamometer or force plate. Every rehab talk should state what to do without one.
- **Late presentation** — kabaddi ACL injuries present at a mean of ~14 months. Programme it as a clinical reality, not a footnote.

## 12. Industry involvement & CPD independence

*Added Sep 2026. Corrects an assumption made during planning: that product-branded sessions would break TSMC CPD accreditation. No published TGMC rule saying so was found. This section separates what Indian rules actually require from international best practice.*

**What the Indian rules say**

- **NMC's Registered Medical Practitioner (Professional Conduct) Regulations 2023 are in abeyance** (notified 2 Aug 2023, held in abeyance 23 Aug 2023); the **Indian Medical Council (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics) Regulations 2002** still apply. [NMC notice](https://www.nmc.org.in/rules-regulations/national-medical-commission-registered-medical-practitioner-professional-conduct-regulations-2023-reg/)
- **IMC 2002, clause 6.8** (added 2009) binds *individual doctors*: no gifts, travel as a delegate, hospitality or cash from industry; affiliations must be "fully transparent and disclosed"; a doctor "shall not endorse any drug or product of the industry publically". It says nothing about session titles, booths or CPD accreditation. [Regulations text (PDF)](https://wbconsumers.gov.in/writereaddata/ACT%20&%20RULES/Relevant%20Act%20&%20Rules/Code%20of%20Medical%20Ethics%20Regulations.pdf) -- some online copies number clause 6.8 differently.
- **TGMC (TSMC) CPD.** Verifiable: CPD Committee minutes of 18 Feb 2024 -- 2 credit hours per 8-hour day, Rs 50,000 permission fee for a national conference, observers may attend ([minutes](https://onlinetsmc.in/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/cpd-mom.jpg)); the organiser declaration -- cooperate with the TGMC observer, at least one ethics / good clinical practice / legal session, consider free registration for doctors over 65, written consent from every workshop patient ([declaration](https://onlinetsmc.in/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/CPD-Declaration.pdf)). **No TGMC rule on product names, sponsorship or disclosure format was found.**
- **Direction of travel.** NMC is reported (Oct 2025) to be moving against sponsored CME and product marketing during live surgery, naming Hyderabad hotel events; NMC's own text was not found. [Telangana Today](https://telanganatoday.com/nmc-set-to-crack-down-on-five-start-cme-events-and-pharm-sponsorship-for-live-surgeries)
- **UCMPMD 2024** (Department of Pharmaceuticals code) binds *device companies*: transparent, verifiable CME-support procedures with disclosure of spend; travel and hospitality for speakers only; demonstration products allowed but taken back; brand reminders capped at INR 1,000. Source is a law-firm summary, not the code text -- verify. [Summary (PDF)](https://www.cyrilshroff.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Client-Alert-FAQs-on-UCMPMD-2024.pdf)

**Practical rules for SIC**

*Required by Indian rules:*
- No faculty member publicly endorses a product. A podium "technique highlight" of a named system risks clause 6.8 (an interpretation; no ruling found).
- No industry-paid delegate travel, hospitality, gifts or cash to individual doctors, including prizes. Booth giveaways sit awkwardly with the doctor-side rule even though UCMPMD lets companies give small brand reminders.
- Faculty industry relationships disclosed.
- TGMC: the ethics / GCP plenary (Day 1, 17:32 -- already in the grid), cooperation with the observer, the over-65 registration question (Section 7, item 14), written consent for any workshop patient, application and payment through the portal.
- Company booth demonstrations are permitted; no Indian rule governs where they sit.

*Best practice (ACCME Standards for Integrity and Independence; EACCME live-event criteria -- not binding in India):* [ACCME](https://accme.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/881_20220623_Standards-for-Integrity-and-Independence-in-Accredited-CE-Information-Package.pdf) - [EACCME](https://eaccme.uems.eu/web/content/slide.slide/30/binary_content?download=true)
- Generic names only in session titles and on slides; no logos in Halls A or B.
- A disclosure slide opening every talk (name, company, nature of relationship, or "none"); the chair shows funding sources at the opening session.
- Live demos and technique theatres only in the exhibition area during tea and lunch, labelled "Industry -- not part of the CPD programme" and excluded from CPD hours; nothing commercial inside either hall within 30 minutes of a session.
- Sponsors named on the website, schedules and an acknowledgements page -- no per-session branding, branded lanyards or hall advertising.
- A written sponsor agreement: sponsors have no say on content or faculty; no industry employees as faculty in accredited sessions.
- If a technique slot is wanted inside the programme: independent faculty comparing several systems under generic names.

*Applies to research in this document:* several cited papers name commercial products in their titles or abstracts -- cite them by author, journal and DOI only on slides.
